I study how social experience and the brain’s functional organization relate to cognition across the lifespan, from childhood development to aging and neurodegenerative disease.

What first fascinated me about sociology was how our thoughts and behaviors are shaped not only by individual characteristics, but also by the relationships and environments around us. As I studied social networks, I became increasingly curious about how such relationships and experiences influence individuals. This led me to a broader question: why do people think and respond differently, even when they are in similar environments?

That curiosity prompted me to expand my research into neuroscience. While sociology taught me to understand people within their social contexts, neuroscience offered new ways to investigate the neural processes associated with these experiences and individual differences. I see the two fields not as separate paths, but as complementary perspectives that together offer a more complete understanding of human behavior.

Today, by combining large-scale neuroimaging cohort analyses with behavioral and fMRI experiments, I aim to connect different types of data and disciplinary perspectives to explore relationships that might be difficult to uncover through any single approach.

Current projects

Social support, brain organization, and cognitive aging

KAIST, Cognitive Neuroscience & NeuroImaging Lab

Analyzing ADNI, AABC, and KBASE cohorts with fMRI, DTI, and PET to study brain organization in aging and Alzheimer’s disease, cognitive reserve, and the possible role of social support.

How children and older adults read emotional faces

Korea Brain Research Institute, Brain Development Imaging Lab

Running fMRI, survey, and behavioral studies with children and caregivers, and building the Korean Older Adult Facial Expression Database (KOFED) and the Facial Expression Recognition Task (FERT).
